Baghbaan: research and integration plan

An all-in-one app for Kashmir's apple growers: what it should contain, and which tools, data, technologies and partners to build it with.

Companion to the Baghbaan demo app walkthrough (PDF) and the clickable prototype (claude.ai/artifact/T7wV1p9vo63Sqa659bEQCG; source in prototype/). Prepared November 2026.

How to read this report. Part 1 is the recommendation: what the app should contain and how each part works. Part 2 catalogues what we can integrate: data sources and APIs, technologies, datasets, apps to learn from, competitors and regulations. Part 3 covers architecture, business model, roadmap and risks. Appendices A–C hold the full desk research with a source link next to each fact. Facts marked [V] were read at the source, [S] come from search summaries and should be re-checked, and UNVERIFIED items are leads only. Crop-protection content in section 4 is general practice and must be checked against the current SKUAST-K Package of Practices before anything reaches growers.

Summary

  • The opportunity is large and concentrated. Jammu & Kashmir grew 21.10 lakh tonnes of apple on 1.73 lakh hectares in 2024-25, roughly 70–80% of India's apples. Seven to seven and a half lakh families and about 35 lakh people depend on horticulture, worth ₹10,000–12,000 crore a year (J&K Economic Survey 2025-26; Assembly reply, Sept 2026; Appendix B §1).
  • Growers lose money at five predictable points: disease (2026 was a bad scab year, with B/C grades selling at ₹200–250 a box at Sopore against ₹1,000–1,300 for premium fruit), spurious inputs, highway closures (the 26 Aug–17 Sep 2025 NH-44 closure stranded 2,000+ trucks; traders put losses at ₹500–700 crore and freight jumped from ₹70–100 to ₹200–300 a box), weak price information, and labour and transport at harvest. There is no crop insurance for apple today (scheme on hold, Sept 2026) (Appendix B §2–5).
  • Recommendation: build Baghbaan around one question, "what is wrong with my orchard and what do I do?" The home screen is the Orchard Doctor, a Yes / No and picture-based diagnosis that works offline. Around it sit Buy (licensed-dealer marketplace), Learn (short Kashmiri-audio lessons), Mandi & news (rates, where-to-sell, highway, rail, weather, advisories) and Services (trucks, labour, spray teams, equipment, experts, cold storage). My orchard quietly builds a spray diary and orchard record from what growers already do.
  • Design for the real conditions: voice and pictures first; Kashmiri voice with Urdu-script text; offline-first with SMS and a missed-call voice line, because J&K has had more internet shutdowns than any other part of India; no signup form, and data asked only when it improves an answer.
  • Integrate first: SKUAST-K's spray schedule as the advice rule base; data.gov.in / Agmarknet mandi prices; IMD and a commercial weather API for spray windows and a Mills-type scab model; Bhashini and IndicTrans2 for Kashmiri; Kashmir-specific CC BY 4.0 leaf-image datasets for a later photo model; J&K Traffic Police advisories plus driver reports for roads; and partnerships with the Department of Horticulture's Kisan Khidmat Ghars for assisted onboarding.
  • Competition exists but leaves the core gap open. Orchardly (claims 15,000+ growers), Farmit by Qul, Apple Doc and others offer advisory or IoT. None found offers Kashmiri voice, a neutral licensed-dealer marketplace, and labour and transport booking in one app (Appendix C §2).
  • Start with a two-district pilot (Shopian and Baramulla/Sopore, the two largest dispatch mandis), earn trust with free diagnosis, rates and alerts, then add the marketplace and credit.

Part 1 · What the app should contain

1. The grower and the problem

Who uses it. Owners of small family orchards (often 5–40 kanal; 1 hectare ≈ 19.8 kanal), many over fifty, many more comfortable speaking Kashmiri than reading any script; their sons and daughters who handle the phone; younger agriculture graduates running new high-density blocks; and the people around them: dealers, transporters, labour leaders, commission agents and extension staff.

Where the money is lost.

Pain point What happens today What the app does
Disease and pests Spray decisions come from the dealer or neighbours; sprays are mistimed relative to rain; the same fungicide group is repeated. Orchard Doctor; weather-driven infection alerts; spray diary with group rotation; dose calculator.
Spurious or wrong inputs Fake or mislabelled packs; products without an apple label claim; over-buying. Licensed-dealer marketplace; brand comparison of the same molecule; genuine-pack QR check; buy-the-right-quantity.
Market information Prices heard from the commission agent; no view across mandis or net of costs. Daily rates by variety and grade; 30-day trends; "where should I sell?" net-of-freight calculator; price alerts.
Highway and logistics NH-44 closures strand loaded trucks; freight spikes; no view of rail options. Highway, Mughal Road and rail status with alerts; truck booking with shared loads; rail parcel option.
Labour and services Phone calls to find teams; unclear rates; trust problems with unknown providers. Verified, rated providers; fair-rate guides; booking and tracking; pay after work.
Knowledge Package of Practices is a long PDF; extension staff are thinly spread. Season-aware three-minute lessons with Kashmiri audio; library; calendar; expert escalation.
Credit Informal credit from the dealer, tied to buying from him and settled at harvest. Pay-after-harvest credit through a regulated lender, linked to the Kisan Credit Card.

3. Home and the Orchard Doctor (the main page)

3.1 Home

The home screen answers "what should I do today?" for this grower's village and altitude:

  • The main question in a green card: "What's troubling your orchard today?" with Start, a microphone (speak the problem in Kashmiri) and a camera (photo check).
  • Quick chips for common entry points: spots on leaves, insects and mites, fruit problem, tree drying, what to spray now.
  • An alert with an action, e.g. "Scab infection risk on Wednesday: 14 mm rain, leaves wet ~18 h at 11–16 °C, your last spray was 9 days ago → Spray before Wednesday noon".
  • Crop stage for this altitude (dormant → green tip → pink bud → bloom → petal fall → fruitlet → growth → harvest → leaf fall) and the next spray due.
  • Four tiles: mandi rate and trend, NH-44 status, today's spray window, free spray teams nearby.
  • One quick question a day (optional, one tap, reason shown): bees seen during bloom? pests seen this week? crop better or worse than last year?

The home content changes with the season: scab and bloom in spring, mites and Alternaria in summer, harvest, trucks and CA storage in autumn, urea on leaves and pruning in late autumn and winter.

3.2 How a diagnosis works

  1. Where is the problem? Leaves, fruit, flowers, branches and bark, roots and whole tree, or "not sure: take a photo".
  2. Yes / No / Not sure questions, each with two pictures: what a Yes looks like and what a No looks like, plus a Listen button.
  3. A live "narrowing down" panel showing the top candidates.
  4. One multiple-choice step for severity (a few trees, about half, most), while the last spray is read from the spray diary rather than asked again.
  5. The result: most likely cause with match strength and the reasons ("velvety olive-green spots; spots on young fruit"), urgency from the forecast, what to do now, products that fit (registered for apple and on the university schedule), how to prevent it next season, look-alikes to rule out, and buttons to ask an expert or save to the diary.

The engine. A small, auditable naive-Bayes model. Each problem has a prior for the season, altitude band and recent nearby reports; each question has the probability of a Yes given each problem, written by experts. After each answer the scores update, and the next question is the one with the largest expected information gain, so most diagnoses end in 2–4 questions. The engine stops at 90% confidence, and hands over to a human when answers conflict, when nothing fits, or for problems that must always go to an expert (root rot, sudden spread, fire-blight-like symptoms). In the demo, two Yes answers move scab from 33% to 91% to 99%.

Getting smarter. Expert confirmations and photo labels from real cases re-estimate the likelihoods each season. The question bank is versioned, and each version is signed off by the content partner.

Photo check (phase 2). An on-phone image model (LiteRT, MobileNet-class) trained on Kashmir field images (Appendix C §3–4) confirms, but does not replace, the questions. Models trained only on PlantVillage lab images drop sharply in accuracy on field photos, so field images must be collected with consent through the app.

Safety rules hard-coded in the engine: suggest only products with a CIB&RC label claim for apple and on the current SKUAST-K schedule; never an insecticide during bloom; never a banned or restricted molecule; show "read the label" with every product; escalate uncertain or high-consequence cases.

Spray-schedule backbone (by stage). The Doctor, the home alerts and the shop all hang off the SKUAST-K schedule's stages: dormant / silver tip (horticultural mineral oil for scale and mite eggs), green tip and pink bud (scab protection), bloom (no insecticides; bees), petal fall and fruitlet (scab, mildew; thinning), fruit development (Alternaria, Marssonina, mites; calcium), pre-harvest, and post-harvest / leaf fall (urea on leaves; sanitation). The schedule is revised yearly and has high-density variants; the app must load the current edition and show its year on every recommendation.

5. Buy: a licensed-dealer marketplace

Categories: fungicides; insecticides; mite control (acaricides); spray oils (HMO); fertilizers (urea, DAP, MOP, NPK, CAN, SSP); micronutrients (boron, zinc, calcium); organic and biological (vermicompost, FYM, neem, Trichoderma, biofertilizers); tools and sprayers; hail nets and supports; packing (cartons, trays); saplings and rootstocks (NHB-accredited nurseries); safety kits (PPE).

What makes it different from a general agri shop:

  • Kits matched to the orchard: stage + area + sprayer drum + last spray group → the right products in the right quantity.
  • Filter by problem and stage, not by brand. Each card shows the FRAC/IRAC group, the label claim and whether the group was used last time.
  • Same molecule, several brands: price per pack across licensed dealers.
  • Dose calculator in local units (kanal, trees, 200-litre drums).
  • Genuine-pack check: scan the pack QR, match batch and expiry to the manufacturer's record (needs manufacturer data partnerships; start with the companies that already print QR codes). Failed checks go to the department and the dealer.
  • Safety: PPE, waiting period before harvest, bee and water warnings on every product.
  • Fulfilment: pickup at the village dealer (cheap and keeps dealers on side) or delivery.
  • Payment: UPI, cash at pickup, and pay-after-harvest credit through a regulated lender.
  • Reviews only from verified buyers, shown with district.

Compliance. Pesticides may be sold only by licensed sellers; run the shop as a marketplace for licensed dealers, store each dealer's licence and show it on orders, and sell only products registered for apple. National platforms already operate this way. Fertilizer sales follow the Fertilizer Control Order through authorised dealers; urea and DAP have government-fixed MRPs. Confirm the current online-sale conditions for both with a lawyer (Appendix C §7, flagged UNVERIFIED there).

8. Services: a trusted marketplace for help

What growers can book: trucks (open, shared part-loads, refrigerated), rail parcel space, labour teams (pruning, spraying, thinning, harvest, grading and packing), spray services with power sprayers, tractors and equipment, expert visits, soil-test sample pickup, CA cold storage, beehives, certified nursery plants, packing material, and buyers for produce.

Trust and safety (patterns from Indian platforms, Appendix A §4):

  • ID and document checks (driving licence, RC, insurance for trucks); police-registration status for non-local labour, which J&K Police requires.
  • Ratings from verified bookings only; "jobs done" counts.
  • Fair-rate guides by district and season, with the government transport corporation's freight as a benchmark.
  • PIN-closed jobs (the grower enters a code when work is done); pay after work; dispute desk.
  • Shared loads: pool part-loads going to the same mandi on the same night.
  • Live trip status tied to highway alerts.

Models to learn from: Bharat Intelligence (WhatsApp/voice booking of labour gangs), the government CHC farm-machinery app (PIN-closed jobs), Vahak and BlackBuck (load boards), ColdKonnect (cold-storage listings). Labour and transport aggregators fall under the Social Security Code's platform-worker contribution (1–2% of turnover, capped at 5% of payouts).

9. My orchard and collecting information without being invasive

Rules.

  1. Value before questions. Every screen works in guest mode. The app helps first, then asks.
  2. One question at a time, at the moment it improves an answer, with "why we ask" and a Skip.
  3. Never ask twice. Reuse what the grower already did: a purchase becomes a spray-diary entry; a diagnosis records the variety and block.
  4. Never ask for Aadhaar, bank passwords, OTPs or land papers.
  5. Aggregate only. Pest and crop reports leave the phone as counts by area.
  6. Reciprocity. A shared report brings back a village alert.
  7. Shared phones. A "helping someone" mode for family members, with the grower's own consent. Phone ownership is lower among women in rural J&K (58% vs 80% for men, 2025 survey), so design for shared devices.

The ladder (when → what we ask → what the grower gets): first open → language, village → local weather, stage, rates. First diagnosis → where, how many trees → a cause and plan. First purchase → orchard size, drum size → exact quantity. Each spray → product, date (from the order) → rotation, reminders, records. Daily, optional → one tap (bees, pests) → village alerts. Season end → crop better or worse → area estimate. Only if they want → phone, name → call-backs, bookings, credit.

Law. India's DPDP Act 2023 and DPDP Rules 2025: consent-manager provisions from about November 2026, main obligations from about May 2027. The app needs an itemised plain-language consent notice (in Kashmiri, Urdu, Hindi, English), withdrawal as easy as consent, breach reporting within 72 hours, log retention, a grievance channel, and verifiable parental consent for users under 18. Penalties reach ₹250 crore (Appendix C §7.1).

10. Designing for Kashmir's growers and networks

  • Pictures and voice first. In studies, low-literacy users completed no tasks with text interfaces but most with voice or picture-based ones; illiterate farmers preferred audio-only advice (Appendix A §1.1).
  • Kashmiri voice, Urdu-script text. Kashmiri speech recognition and synthesis are still limited (Bhashini lists Kashmiri; its Kashmiri TTS is Devanagari-script only), so pre-record prompts with native speakers and use Urdu script for reading.
  • Offline-first. J&K has had several hundred internet shutdowns, including 550 days of no or partial mobile internet from August 2019. Diagnosis, lessons, schedules and last-known rates live on the phone; questions and orders queue; SMS keywords and a missed-call voice line are backups.
  • Simple screens. Large targets, few levels, a replay button, Western digits for prices, long pauses before voice input stops.
  • Assisted channels. Kisan Khidmat Ghars (2,000+ J&K farmer service centres), village helpers, dealers and FPOs as onboarding points. In GSMA's study of the IFFCO Kisan app, a signup profile form put off first-time users and most left within two months; avoid that.

16. Architecture

  • App: Android first (Flutter or React Native), small install, offline database (SQLite), background sync, works on 2 GB RAM phones; a light PWA for sharing links.
  • Content: a headless CMS with an expert review workflow: question bank, likelihood tables, lessons, products, advisories, each versioned and signed off.
  • Doctor engine: shared rules library that runs identically on the phone and the server (the prototype's engine is ~60 lines of JavaScript).
  • Data services: price ingestion (data.gov.in + reporters), weather ingestion (IMD + commercial API), road-status desk, risk models.
  • Channels: WhatsApp Business API, SMS gateway, IVR in Kashmiri, Urdu and Hindi; an expert desk web app for advisors and the department.
  • Marketplace: dealer and provider onboarding with document checks; orders, bookings, PIN-closed jobs; payments through a licensed payment aggregator; lender integration for credit.
  • Privacy: consent records, data minimisation, encryption at rest and in transit, data in India, role-based access for experts.

0. The 15 most actionable design rules (with evidence)

  1. Don't build a text-first app. Lead with pictures and voice, and offer text as an optional layer. - In a mobile-banking test, no low-literacy subject could complete a transaction with a text interface. 72% completed it with spoken dialogue and 100% with a graphical UI. [V] https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/ToCHI2711_Medhi.pdf - In FarmChat, every illiterate participant preferred the audio-only interface. 16 of the literate or semi-literate participants preferred audio plus text. The authors recommend a hybrid: two big buttons plus an optional chat-history view. [V] https://ubicomplab.cs.washington.edu/pdfs/farmchat.pdf
  2. Keep navigation flat. - Avoid menu hierarchies, scrolling, soft-key mappings and non-numeric text entry. - 56 of 90 low-literacy subjects could not navigate hierarchical menus. 48 of 90 did not understand scroll bars. [V] (Medhi et al., link above) - In FarmChat, digitally-illiterate users never scrolled. [V] (FarmChat PDF)
  3. Use voice, replay and pictures, with some specifics. - Write numbers with English (Western) digits. In FarmChat, 11 of 23 literate or semi-literate users could not read Hindi numerals. - Show photos of the product package. Illiterate users valued these because they fear being sold the wrong pesticide. - Put a big "replay" button on every answer. - Allow 7–8 seconds of silence before speech input stops listening. [V] (FarmChat PDF)
  4. Push short, scheduled advisories and let farmers choose how often. - When Gujarat farmers chose their own call frequency, pickup rose 7.6 percentage points (control 80.0%) and listening rose 9.4 pp (control 32.6%). [V] https://registry.precisiondev.org/registry_entry/frequency-a-b-test - A heads-up SMS 10 minutes before an advisory call raised pickup by 5.9 pp and listening by 4 pp in Odisha (about 32,000 users). [V] https://registry.precisiondev.org/registry_entry/impact-of-heads-up-sms-on-call-pickup-and-listening/
  5. Give value before asking for anything. Ask for each data item only when it is needed. - In GSMA's case study of the IFFCO Kisan app, the drop-down profiling step was "a barrier for first time users". Nearly 80% of users churned within two months. GSMA recommended simplified profiling, auto-filling location, and personalising so users see the value of sharing. [V] https://www.gsma.com/mobilefordevelopment/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/IFFCO-Kisan-Agricultural-App.pdf - 18% of shoppers abandon checkout when forced to create an account. [V] https://baymard.com/blog/reduce-cart-abandonment
  6. Ask for permissions and consent in context. - Ask at the moment the user starts the feature that needs it. Explain why, allow "not now", and degrade gracefully. [V] https://developer.android.com/training/permissions/requesting - The DPDP Act requires consent to be "free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous", and withdrawal must be about as easy as giving consent. [V] https://dpdpa.com/dpdpa2023/chapter-2/section6.html

[V] https://kashmirreader.com/2026/01/19/hadp-ushers-a-new-era-of-agricultural-transformation-across-jk/

[V] (FarmChat PDF) - The DPDP Rules let a person nominate someone to exercise their data rights. [V] https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2025/nov/doc20251117695301.pdf - Proposal: an explicit "helper" role, with the grower's own voice or PIN consent.

1. Designing for low-literacy, rural, multilingual farmers

1.1 Text vs graphics vs voice vs a human operator

  • Medhi et al. (ACM TOCHI 2011) [V] https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/ToCHI2711_Medhi.pdf
  • Ethnography with 90 low-literacy subjects in India, Kenya, the Philippines and South Africa, plus 2 quantitative studies with more than 70 subjects in India.
  • "textual interfaces are unusable by first-time low-literacy users, and error prone for literate but novice users."
  • Mobile banking: no low-literacy subject completed a transaction with the text UI. 72% completed it with spoken dialogue and 100% with a graphical UI. Spoken-dialogue users who succeeded were faster and needed less help.
  • Health data entry: a live operator was about 10 times more accurate than text entry.
  • Navigation findings: 56 of 90 could not navigate hierarchical menus (none of the 40 non-phone-users could). 48 of 90 did not understand scroll bars. 45 had trouble with soft keys.
  • Design recommendations:
    • graphical cues;
    • voice annotation;
    • local-language text and audio;
    • minimal hierarchy;
    • no non-numeric text input;
    • no scrolling menus;
    • minimal soft-key mappings;
    • human mediators to introduce the system.
  • Text-free UI (Medhi, Sagar, Toyama, ITID 2007) [S] https://ideas.repec.org/a/tpr/itintd/v4y2007i1p37-50.html
  • Illiterate domestic workers in Bangalore strongly preferred text-free designs.
  • Confirmed the need for semi-abstracted graphics and voice feedback, and a consistent help system.
  • FarmChat (Jain et al., ACM IMWUT 2018) [V] https://ubicomplab.cs.washington.edu/pdfs/farmchat.pdf
  • 34 potato farmers near Ranchi, with a knowledge base built from Kisan Call Centre logs.
  • All illiterate participants preferred audio-only. 16 literate or semi-literate participants preferred audio plus text, mainly to re-read medicine names and doses. 7 preferred audio-only ("nothing to read, so no tension").
  • 16 of 22 digitally-illiterate users preferred audio-only, and none of them scrolled.
  • Physical and environmental factors mattered:
    • sunlight makes the screen unreadable in the field;
    • tractor noise drowns out audio;
    • one older user had shaky hands and could not hit small buttons.
  • Images of medicine packages were highly valued.
  • 11 of 23 literate or semi-literate users could not read Hindi numerals; the authors recommend English digits.
  • Speech input needs a longer silence timeout (7–8 s).
  • Farmers were "highly skeptical" of advice from friends, shopkeepers and agri-apps, so trust has to be earned.
  • Recommended design: a hybrid of two large buttons plus an optional chat-history toggle, with picture-based multiple choice.
  • Agri-entrepreneurs preferred audio plus text for themselves but recommended audio-only for farmers in their village. This is an argument for a separate "assistant mode" UI for intermediaries.

1.2 Video-based extension (Digital Green)

  • 2009 trial [V] https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=156320
  • 13 months, 16 villages, 1,470 households.
  • Digital Green's participatory, locally made videos increased adoption of some practices seven-fold over Training-and-Visit extension.
  • 10 times more effective per dollar on a cost-per-adoption basis.
  • J-PAL RCT in Bihar (2014–2016) [V] https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/evaluation-digital-greens-agricultural-extension-program-india
  • Women farmers in 420 villages.
  • Video did not raise measured System of Rice Intensification (SRI) adoption, possibly because the survey did not capture partial adoption.
  • Bayesian estimates show yields up 12–18% and profits up 9–24%. The video approach was cost-effective: additional profit was about 16 times the extra cost.
  • IFPRI RCT in Ethiopia [S] https://essp.ifpri.info/2022/01/03/accelerating-technical-change-through-icts-evidence-from-a-video-mediated-extension-experiment-in-ethiopia/
  • Reached 24% more farmers and produced up to 44% higher uptake.
  • Cost about $4 per additional adoption. [S] https://bigdata.cgiar.org/digital-intervention/video-enabled-extension/
  • Implication for Kashmir: short, locally filmed Kashmiri-language videos of real growers (scab spray timing, high-density orchard training, grading and packing) are well supported. They can be screened at KKGs or shared over WhatsApp, and should be cached for offline viewing.

1.3 IVR and voice services

  • Avaaj Otalo (Gujarat; CHI 2010) [S] https://hci.stanford.edu/publications/paper.php?id=144
  • Voice forum used by 51 farmers over a 7-month pilot. The most popular feature was the Q&A forum where farmers ask questions and browse others' questions and answers.
  • Cole & Fernando (Economic Journal 2021) [S] https://www.atai-research.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/ueaa084.pdf
  • RCT with 1,200 cotton farmers. More than half called the service in the first 7 months.
  • Treated farmers used more effective pesticides and spent less on ineffective or hazardous ones.
  • Trust in phone-based advice rose from near 0 to more than 6 on a 10-point scale.
  • Yield gains (cumin +28%, cotton +8.6% in a sub-group) are from the working paper. UNVERIFIED whether the published version keeps them.
  • Peer-effects follow-up: [S] https://voxdev.org/topic/agriculture/mobile-phone-based-extension-peer-interactions-and-farmer-information-exchange
  • Awaaz.De (Avaaj Otalo's successor)
  • Its Khedut Saathi service let subscribers forward episodes to up to 5 friends (viral voice sharing). [S] https://www.slideshare.net/awaazde/khedut-saathi-viral-voice-messaging-for-small-farmers-in-india
  • Gram Vaani / Mobile Vaani [V] https://solve.mit.edu/solutions/35346
  • The user gives a missed call and automatically receives a free call-back; navigation is by keypad.
  • Users can record messages; 3–4% of calls produce a recording. Machine learning plus human moderators filter content.
  • Scale: more than 100,000 monthly unique users before COVID-19 and more than 10,000 calls per day (self-reported).
  • Bihar evaluation: only about 23% of interviewed women had heard of Mobile Vaani. [S] https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/12/10/e052336.full
  • A related Uttar Pradesh study found that how many messages women heard depended on phone ownership, ease of phone access and women's empowerment. [S] https://files.comminit.com/content/development-mhealth-behavior-change-communication-strategy-case-study-rural-uttar-pradesh
  • Lesson: voice channels need offline outreach, especially to reach women.
  • IFFCO Kisan "Green SIM" [V] (GSMA PDF)
  • 3–4 daily push voice messages.
  • Average pickup rate 38–52% across 1.8 million active users; average listening about 42 seconds.
  • Implication: an IVR line with missed-call call-back and short (under 1 minute) push voice messages in Kashmiri. It reaches basic-phone owners and keeps working when mobile data is cut, as long as voice is up.

1.4 WhatsApp and AI chat services in Indian agriculture

  • Digital Green FarmerChat (WhatsApp, Telegram and web; generative AI over Digital Green's video transcripts and call-centre logs) [V] https://www.isdm.org.in/ekam/development-management-hub/ask-and-they-receive
  • More than 225,000 users, more than 160,000 of them in India; more than 20 languages.
  • Voice queries are 5 times text queries.
  • 30% of queries are about pests and diseases.
  • Features added from farmer feedback: text-to-speech, audio answers, follow-up questions, a weather widget.
  • Early rollout went through extension agents first, which added a human review step. [S] https://openai.com/index/digital-green

  • Kisan e-Mitra (PM-KISAN scheme chatbot, launched 21 Sept 2023) [V] https://apolitical.co/en/navigator/case-studies/kisan-e-mitra-indias-voice-enabled-ai-support-for-farmers
  • Voice or typed questions in 11 languages, aiming for all 22.
  • More than 20,000 queries per day (April 2025).
  • Uses Bhashini for language. It can answer personal status questions because it is linked to structured beneficiary records.
  • Farm Vaidya (Telugu voice AI agents; IFPRI was technical adviser) [S] https://www.ifpri.org/blog/generative-ai-powered-voice-technology-in-agricultural-advisory-services-lessons-from-india/
  • Farmers' local names for crops and pests are missing from standard datasets, so the team collected real farm speech to build speech-to-text.
  • Agents must adapt to the crop season.
  • Bharat Intelligence (Nashik farm-labour platform) [V] https://thebetterindia.com/innovation/ai-farm-labour-platform-bharat-intelligence-labour-os-nashik-12108095
  • Booking happens on WhatsApp and by voice call. "There's no app to download."
  • Implication: a WhatsApp number that accepts voice notes and photos and replies with voice notes is a low-friction second channel. Treat it as a front door into the same back end as the app. AI answers for scab or pest advice should be reviewed by experts until they are proven accurate.

3.1 Progressive profiling and value before asking

  • IFFCO Kisan (GSMA 2016) [V] https://www.gsma.com/mobilefordevelopment/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/IFFCO-Kisan-Agricultural-App.pdf

  • "the drop down profiling can be a barrier for first time users."

3.2 Just-in-time permissions and consent

  • Android guidance [V] https://developer.android.com/training/permissions/requesting

  • "Don't block the user."
  • Degrade gracefully if denied.
  • "persistent nagging to reconsider is not respectful of their choice."
  • Denying twice counts as a permanent denial, so ask only at the moment of need.

3.5 DPDP Act 2023 and DPDP Rules 2025 (brief)

Rules and timeline - Notified 14 Nov 2025, with an 18-month phased compliance period. [V] https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2025/nov/doc20251117695301.pdf - Most obligations (Rules 3 and 5–16, including notice) apply about 13 May 2027. Consent-manager registration applies after 12 months. - [S] https://www.mondaq.com/india/data-protection/1713500/regulatory-update-notification-of-the-digital-personal-data-protection-rules-2025 ; [S] https://www.storyboard18.com/amp/digital/breaking-govt-notifies-final-dpdp-rules-sets-staggered-rollout-with-key-obligations-for-data-fiduciaries-84201.htm . The exact day is UNVERIFIED (13 or 14 May 2027).

Consent (s.6(1)) [V] https://dpdpa.com/dpdpa2023/chapter-2/section6.html - Must be "free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous with a clear affirmative action". - Limited to the data necessary for the stated purpose. - Withdrawal must be about as easy as giving consent (s.6(4)).

Notice (s.5) [V] https://dpdpa.com/dpdpa2023/chapter-2/section5.html - Must state the personal data and the purpose, how to exercise rights, and how to complain to the Data Protection Board. - Must offer the notice "in English or any language specified in the Eighth Schedule". The Eighth Schedule includes Kashmiri, Urdu and Hindi; AI4Bharat lists Kashmiri (ks) among the "22 officially recognized languages of India". [V] https://huggingface.co/ai4bharat/indic-conformer-600m-multilingual

Rule 3 [V] https://www.dpdpa.com/dpdparules/rule3.html - The notice must be understandable independently of other information. - It must give an itemised description of the data and the purposes. - It must include a website or app link and other means to withdraw consent with comparable ease, exercise rights, and complain to the Board.

Legitimate use without consent (s.7(a)) [V] https://dpdpa.com/dpdpa2023/chapter-2/section7.html - A business may process data that a person voluntarily provided for a specified purpose, unless she indicated she does not consent. The Act's example is a pharmacy texting a payment receipt. - This plausibly covers using a phone number to confirm a booking the grower requested. That is my interpretation and needs legal review.

Children (s.9) [V] https://dpdpa.com/dpdpa2023/chapter-2/section9.html - Under-18s need verifiable parental consent. - No tracking, behavioural monitoring or targeted advertising directed at children. - This matters because younger family members often operate the phone for an elderly grower.

Rights and duties under the Rules [V] (PIB link above) - Rights to access, correct, update and erase, and to nominate another person to act on one's behalf. - Data-rights requests must be answered within 90 days. - Breaches must be notified to affected people in plain language "without delay". - Organisations must display a contact person for data queries. - Consent managers must be India-based. - Penalties: up to Rs 250 crore for failing to maintain reasonable security safeguards; up to Rs 200 crore for breach-notification or child-related violations; up to Rs 50 crore for other violations.

Proposal: an audio consent notice in Kashmiri (with an Urdu/Hindi/English text version), itemising each data item next to its picture. Add a "Delete my data / Stop" option reachable within two taps, or via IVR or a KKG.

5.2 Input credit and "pay after harvest"

  • Gramophone with Jai Kisan (2022) [V] https://agfundernews.com/gramophone-evolves-from-marketplace-to-full-stack-platform-with-new-lending-solutions-for-farmers
  • Underwriting used farm size, crops, irrigation and transaction history collected during advisory.
  • Eligible farmers got an input purchase limit. They paid interest and fees upfront and repaid the principal after harvest.
  • Jai Kisan
  • Acquired an NBFC licence via Kushal Finnovation. [S] https://inc42.com/buzz/jai-kisan-secures-nbfc-licence-after-picking-up-stake-in-kushal-finnovation
  • Co-lends with banks, including public-sector banks. [S] https://inc42.com/buzz/rural-fintech-startup-jai-kisan-nets-inr-26-5-cr-from-existing-investors/
  • farMart
  • Credit linked to input purchases at partner retailers, repaid in a lump sum at harvest. [S] https://bfaglobal.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/About-farMart.pdf
  • Its cashless model worked locally but could not scale without collection staff, and it shifted to merchants. [V] https://inc42.com/?p=235037
  • DeHaat planned input credit based on members' transaction history. Old source; current status UNVERIFIED. [S] https://agfundernews.com/?p=12335
  • Arya.ag lends against warehouse receipts. It partnered with NBFC MAS Financial for small farmers and farmer producer organisations (FPOs). [S] https://agritimes.co.in/investment-finance/arya-ag-tie-up-with-mas-financial-services-to-offer-agri-finance-to-small-farmers-and-fpos
  • J&K: the Kisan Sathi portal routes credit applications to banks digitally. [V] https://kashmirreader.com/2026/01/19/hadp-ushers-a-new-era-of-agricultural-transformation-across-jk/
  • Why agri-fintech struggled (Inc42, Dec 2020) [V] https://inc42.com/?p=235037
  • Thin data: banks want 4–5 years of history; land records are outdated.
  • Cash repayments are hard to scale.
  • An NBFC licence takes more than a year, so startups operate as business correspondents and lenders ask for 20–40% guarantees.
  • Local intermediaries lend in about 5 days vs about 20 days for government loans.
  • Informal borrowing rises mid-season, when unplanned costs hit.
  • Implication for Kashmir (proposal)
  • Input credit should run only through a regulated partner (a bank or NBFC, possibly J&K Bank or KCC-linked).
  • Repayment should be tied to apple sale proceeds, for example through commission-agent settlement or CA-storage receipts, the Arya.ag analogue.
  • This needs collections and legal design before launch.

  • Ninjacart cut low-margin (1–2%) FMCG trading. [S] https://www.theweek.in/wire-updates/business/2025/12/26/dcm46-biz-agri-ninjacart.html
  • Trringo was absorbed into its parent with a net worth of Rs 1.82 cr. [V] (link in 4.3)
  • Funding collapse: agritech funding fell 78% to $178M in 2023, rose about 30% in 2024, then fell 58% year on year in H1 2025 to $96M (Tracxn-type data; other trackers differ). [S] https://entrackr.com/report/indias-agritech-at-inflection-point-funding-revival-profitable-models-and-consolidation-11036024
  • Industry analysis: the early direct-to-farmer model assumed scale would make up for low margins, but the opposite held. Durable businesses address productivity, logistics and finance. [S] https://yourstory.com/2026/04/what-happened-when-scale-met-the-farm-reality
  • Offline-heavy operations are the norm (EM3) [V], and agent models are costly (GSMA) [S].

Implications for the Kashmir app (proposal) 1. Earn from transactions that already involve money and pain: freight, labour gangs, CA space, spraying services. Use a small, transparent booking fee or a provider subscription (the ColdKonnect model). 2. Keep advisory free; it drives acquisition and trust. 3. Use the KKG network for distribution rather than paid customer acquisition. 4. Add credit only with a regulated partner. 5. Measure repeat bookings per season, not downloads.

2.3 How price discovery works

  • Players. The Shopian (Aglar) and Sopore APMCs connect growers, orchard contractors (pre-harvest buyers of the standing crop), packing-house operators, commission agents (arhtiyas) and transporters. Commission agents give growers credit in advance and keep a percentage of the sale value. This creates a credit dependency that weakens growers' bargaining power when supply is congested (SKUAST-K preprint, Oct 2026, citing Mir & Mir 2018, Gulati et al. 2018 and GoJK 2021).
  • Consignment model. Much of the crop is consigned through local forwarding or commission agents to agents in Azadpur and other terminal markets. The price is set at the terminal auction, and freight and commission are deducted from the grower's proceeds. A NABARD draft, reported by DNA (year unclear and dated), estimated about 70% of output is "captive supply" tied to agents' credit (search summary, UNVERIFIED). Commission is often cited at about 6% at Azadpur, but that figure is from a 2010 Himachal report (Tribune 2010, search summary). The current rate in Kashmir is UNVERIFIED.
  • Private channels. Some private companies run their own warehouses or CA stores and pricing schemes. They bypass the mandis and commission agents and pay premium prices for top grades, with strict grading (USDA FAS, Nov 2026).
  • How prices travel. Shopian chains adjust to Azadpur prices about twice as fast as Sopore chains. The mean producer-to-terminal spread was about Rs 9.74/kg over 2015–2025, peaking at Rs 46.70/kg during disruptions (SKUAST-K preprint).
  • Grades used in the trade. A, B and C grades. Premium fruit is packed as "char tahi" (four layers) and "do tahi" (two layers) (Rising Kashmir, 3 Nov 2026). Common varieties: Delicious (Royal/Red Delicious), American (Richared), Kullu (Kulu) Delicious, Maharaji, Hazratbali, and HD varieties such as Gala.

1.4 Lessons from section 1 (Analysis)

  1. Government apps lose trust through broken registration and login, empty data screens and stale prices (Meghdoot, Mausam, e-NAM, Agmarknet 2.0, Kisan Khidmat Ghar). Allow use without registration, use OTP-only login, and show a fallback when an API returns nothing.
  2. Never hard-fail when location permission is denied. Offer a district or village picker (Mausam complaint).
  3. Language must be switchable independently of location (AgroStar and DeHaat complaints). None of the national apps checked offers Kashmiri.
  4. E-commerce ratings collapse on wrong items, stock errors and unanswered returns (BigHaat).
  5. Expert escalation within an hour is valued (Orchardly reviews, section 2). DeHaat promises 30 minutes.
  6. Weather-only apps rate poorly. Value comes from translating weather into actions (spray or don't spray, frost or hail alerts).
